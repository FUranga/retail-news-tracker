# Retail Wire — Drafting reference

Reference knowledge for drafting retail news stories from LSE press releases and other sources, following the Bloomberg method and Retail Week house style.

## The four-paragraph lead

1. **Theme (the lead).** What happened AND why it matters ("what... so what"). Under 20 words where possible. Lead with the newsworthy name — names make news. Only the single most newsworthy figure belongs here; a pile of numbers distracts the reader. Be assertive and useful — never announce in the lead what you don't yet know.
2. **Details.** The facts that support the theme. Dose it — an avalanche of figures scares the reader off. Use editorial judgement on what belongs up top versus later in the piece.
3. **Quote.** A plain-English statement from someone with real stake in the story, backing the theme. If no usable quote exists in the source material, say so explicitly rather than inventing one.
4. **Nut graph.** What's at stake. Who wins, who loses. Where does this go next — better or worse, and how? Test: could this paragraph have been written yesterday? If yes, it hasn't earned its place — it must reflect something specific to today's news.

## Headlines

Bloomberg standard: **64 characters max**. A good headline has at least two or three of:
- **Names** — newsworthy names go first.
- **Surprise** — the first four words must hook the reader.
- **What's at stake** — why the story matters.
- **Conflict and resolution** — frame around action and consequence.

## To be sure

Every story tends to favour one side of a conflict (often the dominant investor view). Almost nothing is black and white. Add a short **to-be-sure** section — usually one or two paragraphs — presenting at least one other side.

## Anonymous sources

Used only when necessary. Require at least two people with direct knowledge telling the same story, or flag the piece for extra review. Never quote an anonymous source directly. Critical comments require attribution even when paraphrased.

## Story-type specific angles

**Earnings**: focus on the surprise versus estimates. Always include net income, even in updates. Flag "pro forma", "special", "one-time" or "adjusted" labels as a caution. Compare profit, revenue and EPS to the prior year with percentage change. Mention share/bond price reaction. Make it personal — CEO or major shareholder angle beats a pure numbers piece.

**M&A**: use conditional language until the deal completes — nothing is certain until it is. Cover: what's happening and why, cost, financing, what it means for buyer and seller, competitor reaction, regulatory approval odds, job impact, investor takeaway. Weave in advisers (banks, lawyers) as context, not an afterthought — note what each earns if disclosed.

**Trading updates**: lead with the percentage change to keep numbers to a minimum in the lead itself; put the actual last price somewhere in the first four paragraphs.

**Financing / bond sales / repurchases**: cover amount, why now, intended use, and whether investors are likely to see it as a success — that's the bottom line.

## House style — Retail Week

- Terse, straightforward. No padding, no throat-clearing, no scene-setting before the news.
- British English spelling and terminology throughout (e.g. "trading update" not "earnings report", "shares" not "stock" where both are natural, "£" formatting, DD Month YYYY dates).
- Present tense for immediate developments where it reads naturally.
- Don't editorialise beyond what the source material and quotes support.

## Workflow for this Project

When given a press release or news item to draft:
1. Identify the theme — the single most newsworthy fact and why it matters today.
2. Write the headline (≤64 characters).
3. Draft the four-paragraph lead per the structure above.
4. Add a to-be-sure paragraph if the story has a clear counter-view or risk not yet covered.
5. Flag explicitly if no usable quote was available in the source material, rather than fabricating one.
6. Always note the source (company, date, and that it originated from an LSE RNS/press release or named outlet) at the end of the draft, separate from the story text itself.
